The detail

What the Minnesota statute actually says

Minn. Stat. § 13D.04, subd. 1 requires only that a schedule of regular meetings be kept on file, with no fixed advance notice period, so notice_hours is null. Special meetings require 3 days' posted notice under subd. 2.

Primary source: https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/13D.04

A statute that does not require comment does not forbid it. Most councils take comment under their own rules of procedure, and those rules — not the state act — usually decide how long you get and when you sign up.

Practical

How to take part in Minnesota

The Minnesota statute does not create a general right to address a public body. In practice most councils take public comment anyway, under their own rules of procedure. Those local rules, not the state act, are the thing to read.

  1. Find the meeting, not just the body.Power sits in a specific meeting of a specific body on a specific night — a council session, a planning commission, a zoning board. Start from the calendar, not the homepage. The lookup on the Politicz home page takes an address and returns the bodies that govern it.
  2. Read the posted agenda before anything else.The agenda is the legal object. It tells you what will be decided, and it almost always carries the participation instructions: whether there is a sign-up, whether comment is taken on non-agenda items, and whether remote comment is allowed. If a body takes comment, the agenda is where it says so.
  3. Check the notice window.The Minnesota act sets no single fixed notice period for every covered meeting, so the deadline comes from the specific provision that applies — and, in practice, from the body's own posting habit. Watch for items added late.
  4. Sign up the way that meeting asks you to.Sign-up is usually a card at the door, a form on the agenda page, or an email to the clerk before a cutoff. Councils rarely make exceptions for people who did not sign up, and they almost never refuse someone who did. Do the boring step.
  5. Write for the clock, not for the page.Speaking time is short and the limit is set locally, so check the agenda for yours. One concrete ask, one reason it matters on your street, one sentence naming what you want the body to do. Read it aloud once before you go.
  6. Put the same thing in writing.Written comment is entered into the record and survives the meeting. Send it to the clerk before the cutoff even if you plan to speak. In Minnesota the companion transparency statute — the Minnesota Government Data Practices Act — is also how you request the body's own rules of procedure, past minutes and the records behind an agenda item.
