What if taking part
felt worth it?

Politicz begins with a simple belief: the people who live with a decision should have a meaningful way to shape it. Our bigger idea is that better access to knowledge and better tools for listening can make that ordinary. Here is the argument—and the questions it still has to answer.

I The word

Start with the people, the place, and the choices at stake. These older words offer a way to think about politics as something we take part in, together.

IDIŌTĒS
ἰδιώτης · ancient greek

The Greek word can mean a private person, an individual, or a layperson. We use it here as a starting point for a question, not a label for anyone: what would make it easier to move from private concern to shared action?

You can care about your community and still be unsure where to begin. That is a starting point worth designing for.

POLIS · POLITĒS
πόλις · πολίτης · ancient greek

Politics comes from ta politiká — "the things of the citizens," from polis, the place you live. Not campaigns. Not parties. Not the noise. The things of the people who live here.

You already have a stake in the place you live. Participation should help you act on it.

POSSE
latin · to be able

Power is posse — to be able. Not domination; capacity. Every argument that power is dirty ends in the same place: the people who believe it hand their capacity to the people who don't.

A useful kind of power: having more ways to make life better, together.

TO WIN · TO LOVE
the test that fits in one line

Take winning to mean gaining choices, and losing to mean losing them. Then take Forrest Landry's definition — love is that which enables choice — and the two collapse into one measurable question you can put to any bill, budget, hire, or vote:

Whose choices did this open, and whose did it close?

II The argument

Our thesis: a better public life starts with more people being heard.

The end of politics has been announced twice, and both times the claim was that one side had won — Engels expected the state to wither away once class conflict was settled; Fukuyama declared history over once liberal democracy had no rival. Neither arrived, because neither named what politics is actually made of.

It is made of scarcity — not of power, but of bandwidth. Three hundred and forty million people cannot deliberate, so the founders built a compressor. Madison names it in Federalist 10 as the very thing that separates a republic from a democracy: "the delegation of the government, in the latter, to a small number of citizens elected by the rest." We see representation, in part, as a response to the communication limits of 1787 — the speed of a horse, the reach of a press, the size of a room.

Our argument is that many frustrating features of modern politics are reinforced by that compression. A vote is one bit every two years. A poll is an answer to someone else’s question. A campaign is what you build when attention is the scarce good, and outrage is what wins when attention is rationed. A narrow channel can reward spectacle over understanding. We believe a public with more ways to speak and listen could do better.

Shift I
The record becomes readable.What governments actually do is already public and almost entirely unread — agendas, budgets, transcripts, contracts, produced faster than any citizenry could get through them. Machines that read are the first instrument in history that can make the whole record legible to the people it is about. This site is a small experiment in that: a directory of legal sources and public calendars that gives you a place to start.
Shift II
Fewer fights are zero-sum.A large share of politics is the allocation of scarce things. To the degree that automation and abundance make a thing cheap, the argument over who gets it stops being politics and becomes logistics. That does not end disagreement — it moves the disagreement up, from who gets what to what we actually want.
Shift III
Coordination without compression.A public can now do more than be counted. It can read the same record, argue, converge, and state a considered position at a scale that used to require someone to stand in for it. Representatives still make decisions and carry responsibilities. Better public deliberation could help more people shape the choices in front of them.
What ends is not government. What ends is the excuse.
too many, too slow, too uninformed, too far away — every one of those was an argument about bandwidth

What comes back is the part the word always meant. Civic duty framed as a chore is a modern artifact — it is what taking part feels like when your only available move is to be counted once every two years. Athens paid its citizens to sit on juries and treated the day as ordinary life rather than sacrifice. The measure of a working republic is not turnout. It is whether taking part is worth doing.

We shipped version one, and the world copied the pattern: a written constitution, enumerated powers, a bill of rights. That version also excluded most of the people it claimed to speak for, and took two centuries of amendment to partly repair. A second one that is merely more efficient is not an improvement. It has to be built, from the start, to widen what everyone can choose — which is the only definition of winning this institute uses.

How this claim could be wrong

  • If the record becomes legible and still nobody reads it.
  • If coordination at scale arrives and produces only faster, better-organised outrage.
  • If abundance shows up and the fights get worse rather than moving up a level.
  • If, after all of it, taking part still feels like a chore — then the thesis was wrong, and saying so here is the point of writing it down.

What this site refuses to do

  • No feed, no likes, no follower count, no streak.
  • No petition and no pledge signature. A signature is not an act.
  • No party, no candidate, no endorsement, no donation button.
  • No account. Address lookups send your address to the US Census Geocoder and 5 Calls. Politicz does not save your address. Your optional next-step note and color preference are stored only in your browser.
  • No invented numbers. Every figure comes from a named source, and where the record is incomplete it says so instead of filling the gap.
